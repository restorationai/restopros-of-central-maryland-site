## Who do you call first when your basement floods?

Call in this order: handle any electrical hazard, stop the water source if it's safe to reach, then call a restoration company and your insurer at roughly the same time. A flooded basement with water near outlets, a furnace, or the electrical panel is a shock hazard before it's a cleanup problem, so that comes first, every time.

If water is rising near your panel or any outlets, don't wade in. Call Pepco or a licensed electrician to cut power to that circuit before anyone steps into the water. Once it's safe, look for the source: a tripped or burned-out sump pump, a cracked foundation wall after heavy rain, a supply line feeding the house, or a backed-up floor drain. If it's a running supply line you can reach, shutting the main valve stops it from getting worse. If it's a WSSC Water main break outside the house, that's a utility call, not a plumbing one.

Once the source is handled, or if water keeps coming in faster than you can manage, call RestoPros. ## Is a flooded basement covered by homeowners insurance?

Usually not, and the reason depends on where the water came from. A standard homeowners policy typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst supply line, but most policies exclude flood, groundwater seepage, and sump pump failure unless you've added specific coverage.

If your sump pump failed and groundwater flooded the basement, that's usually only covered with a water backup (sump/sewer) endorsement on the policy. The same goes for a sewer or drain backup. If water came in as surface runoff during heavy rain, that's a flood, which standard homeowners policies exclude entirely; it requires a separate policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier. Our guide on [whether homeowners insurance covers water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through these distinctions in more detail. Whatever the source, photograph everything and keep receipts before cleanup starts; a documented loss is easier for an adjuster to evaluate fairly.

## Is basement floodwater Category 2 or Category 3?

It depends on the source, and getting this right changes how the cleanup is handled. Under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), groundwater seeping in through a foundation wall under hydrostatic pressure is Category 2 (gray water), contaminated enough to require careful handling. Surface water flowing in from outside during a storm, and any sewer or drain backup, is Category 3 (black water), the most contaminated classification, which calls for antimicrobial treatment and PPE during cleanup.

This matters for what gets dried versus removed. Category 2 and 3 water usually means carpet padding, baseboards, and sometimes drywall get removed rather than dried in place. If you notice a sewage odor or discoloration along with the standing water, treat it as Category 3 and keep the basement off-limits to kids and pets until a crew has assessed it.
