## What Drives Storm Damage Costs Up or Down?

The biggest cost drivers are how much water got in, how long it sat before extraction started, how many rooms or floors it reached, and whether a fallen tree or wind-torn roof needs emergency stabilization before drying can even start.

- **Water category.** Wind-driven storm rain is typically treated as Category 3 (grossly contaminated) from the outset, since it carries debris and contaminants as it enters, unlike a clean interior supply-line leak, which starts as Category 1 and only degrades the longer it sits. That difference changes how much material has to come out versus dry in place.
- **Response time.** Mold can begin growing on wet materials within [24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so a same-day start keeps the remediation footprint smaller and the invoice lower.
- **Square footage and floors affected.** A soaked basement corner costs far less than water that traveled across two floors through a shared stairwell.
- **Materials involved.** Drywall and blown-in insulation usually need to be removed and replaced in a storm loss; hardwood and tile can often be dried in place with faster response.
- **Tree and debris complexity.** A tree resting on the roof usually requires a tree service, and sometimes an engineer's sign-off, before a restoration crew can safely access the interior, which adds coordination time.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Storm Damage in Maryland?

Most standard Maryland homeowners policies cover sudden wind damage, such as a tree limb through the roof or wind-torn shingles, along with the water intrusion that follows, but they exclude rising floodwater and gradual seepage, which need a separate flood policy.

Wind and hail are named perils on nearly every standard policy, so a tree through the roof or siding torn off in a storm is typically a covered claim, subject to your deductible. The rainwater that comes in through that new opening in the roof is usually covered too, since it stems from the covered wind event, as long as you act promptly to tarp it and stop further intrusion.

What's not covered: surface flooding from an overflowing creek, storm drain backup, or street flooding entering the home. That requires a separate flood policy, typically through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/). Likewise, if storm runoff backs up through a floor drain or a sump pump fails during the storm, that's generally only covered with a separate water backup endorsement, not standard wind coverage. For the general rules on what a standard policy pays for water damage, see our breakdown of [what homeowners insurance covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/). The [Maryland Insurance Administration](https://insurance.maryland.gov/Consumer/Pages/default.aspx) is a good resource for policy-specific questions.

## What Happens During a Storm Damage Restoration Job?

A storm damage job moves through safety stabilization first, then extraction and drying, then documentation for the insurance claim.

1. **Safety assessment and stabilization.** Tarping the roof, boarding broken windows, and shutting off power to any affected circuits before anyone enters.
2. **Water extraction and drying setup.** Pulling standing water and placing air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, following the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for structural drying.
3. **Moisture mapping.** Daily readings on affected materials until they reach a verified dry standard, not just a visual check.
4. **Debris and tree coordination.** Scheduling a licensed tree service when a fallen tree is involved, so the restoration crew can safely access and dry the interior.
5. **Documentation.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope of work that the adjuster can review against the claim.
