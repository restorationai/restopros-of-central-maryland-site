## What certifications should you actually check?

Look for IICRC certification specifically, not just a general claim of being "trained" or "experienced." The [Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification](https://www.iicrc.org/) sets the technical standard the industry works to, and a Water Damage Restoration Technician (WRT) credential means the crew has been trained to that standard, not just shown how to run a blower fan.

## What questions should you ask before you sign anything?

Ask for a written drying plan with target moisture readings before you sign a work authorization, not after. A real plan names the rooms affected, the equipment going in each one, and a target range the materials need to reach, measured with a moisture meter, not guessed by eye.

Beyond the drying plan, four questions separate a documented job from a vague one:

- **"Will you take daily moisture readings and share them with me?"** You should get numbers, not just a verbal "it's drying fine."
- **"What category of water is this?"** A burst supply line is usually Category 1 (clean water), but a line that's been leaking inside a wall for days, or any backup from a drain, is a different category with different handling requirements under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/).
- **"Will you photograph everything before demolition starts?"** This protects your insurance claim.
- **"Do you remove wet materials or just dry them in place?"** The right answer depends on the category of water and how long it sat, and a competent tech will explain why, not just pick one.

If a company can't answer these clearly on the phone, that's a signal before they ever touch your drywall.

## How does a reputable company handle your insurance claim?

A reputable restoration company documents the loss the way your adjuster needs it documented, with photos, moisture logs, and an itemized scope, so your claim doesn't get questioned later. Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a pipe that bursts, but exclude gradual leaks and long-term seepage, per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance). If the loss involves a sump pump failure or a backup from a drain, check whether your policy has a water backup endorsement, because that coverage is usually separate from the base policy.
