## Is This a Job for a Restoration Company, a Plumber, or Both?

Call a restoration company first if the water that caused the mold has already stopped moving; call a plumber first if water is still actively leaking, running, or backing up.

Mold needs standing moisture to grow, so the first question is always whether the source is still active. A slow drain line, a cracked foundation letting in groundwater after a storm, or a sump pump that quietly failed weeks ago can all leave a basement in Twinbrook or King Farm smelling musty long after the original event. If water is currently flowing, shutting it off is the priority; a plumber (or your main water shutoff valve) handles that. Once the source is stopped, the [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) side of the job starts: containment, air filtration, removal of affected materials, and drying the space back to normal moisture levels. RestoPros coordinates both halves when a job needs it, pairing [water cleanup](/services/water-cleanup/) with remediation so one crew owns the whole timeline instead of two companies pointing at each other.

If the moisture source isn't obvious (no recent flood, no visible leak, just a smell), that's a different call. It usually means water is getting in somewhere you can't see, and that's where an inspection comes in before any cleanup starts.

## Mold Inspector or Mold Remediation Company: Which Do You Actually Need?

Call an inspector when you need to find the source or confirm what you're dealing with before spending money; call a remediation company when the mold and its cause are already identified and it's time to remove it.

An inspector's job is diagnostic: moisture mapping with a meter, sometimes air or surface sampling, and a written report. That's the right first step for a musty smell with no visible mold, or for a pre-purchase basement check. A remediation company's job is the physical work: setting up containment, running negative air with HEPA filtration, removing porous materials that can't be cleaned (drywall, insulation, some carpet padding), and cleaning salvageable surfaces per the [ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for professional mold remediation](https://iicrc.org/s520/). Many homeowners skip the separate inspection step when the cause is obvious, a basement flooded, the drywall stayed wet for a week, mold showed up along the baseline. In that case, go straight to remediation and have the crew document conditions on arrival instead of paying for a second visit.

## Can You Just Clean It Up Yourself?

Small, surface-level mold on a hard, non-porous surface is usually fine to handle yourself; mold spreading across drywall, carpet, or framing, or covering more than roughly 10 square feet, is not.

The [EPA's guidance on mold cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) is the baseline most remediation standards build from: small areas on tile, metal, or sealed concrete can often be scrubbed with detergent and water, dried completely, and monitored. The problem in a lot of Rockville basements is that the mold you can see on a baseboard or the back of drywall is rarely the whole story. Finished basements hide a lot: insulation behind the drywall, subfloor under the carpet pad, framing members inside the wall cavity. If a moisture meter shows elevated readings inside the wall, or if the visible patch keeps coming back after you clean it, that's a sign the problem extends further than a DIY wipe-down can reach.
