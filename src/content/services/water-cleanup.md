## What Emergency Water Cleanup actually involves

Emergency water cleanup covers the first critical hours after a pipe failure, appliance leak, sump pump overflow, or storm intrusion. It's different from a routine mop-and-dry job because the goal is arresting damage that's still actively spreading. That means pulling standing water fast with truck-mounted or portable extraction equipment, identifying where water has traveled behind walls and under flooring (not just where it's pooled), and setting air movers and dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of building materials before they warp, delaminate, or start growing mold. Depending on the source, the water may be clean (Category 1), contain contaminants from appliances or groundwater (Category 2), or carry sewage or floodwater (Category 3), and each category changes what gets removed versus dried in place.

## Our process

1. **Source control and water categorization.** Before anything else, the water source gets identified and stopped if it's still active, and the water is categorized to determine what materials can be dried versus what needs to be removed.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water is pulled using pumps and extraction wands, including water trapped under cushion carpet pad, in wall cavities, and beneath engineered flooring where it tends to pool unnoticed.
3. **Drying and moisture monitoring.** Air movers, dehumidifiers, and moisture meters go in to track drying progress across affected materials day by day, following the IICRC S500 water damage drying standard rather than guessing when things "feel dry."
4. **Containment for Category 2/3 water.** If the water is contaminated, affected materials that can't be sanitized get removed, and the work area is contained to keep contamination from spreading to unaffected rooms.
5. **Final checks.** Moisture readings are confirmed against dry industry benchmarks before equipment comes out, so the space isn't signed off while materials are still holding moisture below the surface.

## What separates a good water cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is drying what's visible and missing what isn't. Carpet can look and feel dry on top while the pad underneath stays saturated, and hardwood can cup or crown weeks later if subfloor moisture wasn't actually brought down to a dry standard, just assumed dry because the surface felt fine. Another frequent miss: treating all water the same. Water from a clean supply line doesn't carry the same risk as water that's backed up through a floor drain, and skipping that distinction means contaminated material sometimes gets dried instead of removed. Insurance adjusters reviewing a water claim typically look for daily moisture logs, photos documenting the extent of the water intrusion, and a clear record of what was removed versus dried, since that documentation is what supports the scope of the claim.
