**Water heater burst in Rockville and water is spreading across the floor?** Call now, we answer 24/7. A failed water heater can empty 40 to 80 gallons in minutes, and that water finds its way under flooring, into drywall, and down into basement framing faster than most homeowners expect.

A water heater failure rarely gives warning. Tanks corrode from the inside out, and a slow pinhole leak at the bottom seam can go unnoticed for weeks until the unit finally lets go. Other times a fitting or relief valve fails outright and the tank empties in one event. Either way, the water is usually continuous (the supply line keeps feeding it until someone shuts the valve), which is why water heater floods often cause more damage than a one-time spill.

## What water heater flood cleanup actually involves

This isn't a mop-and-towel job once a tank has emptied into a finished space. A 40-gallon tank failure in a utility closet or basement can soak subflooring, wick up drywall, and pool under baseboards within an hour. If the unit sits on a second floor or in an attic closet, as many townhomes and older Rockville colonials have, the water travels down through ceiling cavities and electrical boxes before it ever reaches a visible drip.

The work starts with stopping the active water source, confirming the supply valve is off, and assessing how far the water traveled before it pooled or was noticed. From there it's extraction of standing water, moisture mapping of the affected materials, and a structural drying setup sized to the actual damage, not a generic template. Because sediment and mineral buildup are common in failed tanks, we also check whether the water carried any contamination that changes how materials are handled.

## Our process

1. **Source confirmation and shutoff verification.** We confirm the water heater supply is fully isolated before any extraction begins, since a leak that looks stopped can still be feeding from a cracked fitting.
2. **Water extraction.** Standing water is pulled from flooring, subfloor cavities, and any lower-level spaces the water reached, using truck-mounted or portable extraction depending on volume.
3. **Moisture mapping.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging identify how far water migrated into drywall, baseboards, subfloor, and framing, since surface-dry materials often hide saturation underneath.
4. **Structural drying and monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed based on the moisture map, and drying progress is checked and logged daily (not assumed) until materials read dry per IICRC S500 standards.
5. **Repair scoping.** Once materials are dry, we document what needs to be removed, replaced, or can be dried in place, so you have a clear written scope before any reconstruction decisions.

## What separates a good water heater flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating a water heater flood like a simple spill: running fans for a day or two and calling it dry. Water heater failures often happen in enclosed spaces (utility closets, under-stair nooks, garage corners) where the water sits against framing and baseboards longer than it would in an open room. If the drying equipment isn't sized to that enclosed cavity, or if the moisture check stops at the surface, the wood framing and subfloor underneath can stay wet for days while everything looks fine from above.

Adjusters reviewing a water heater claim typically want to see documented moisture readings at the start and at completion, not just a before-and-after photo. They also look at whether contamination from tank sediment or standing water was addressed, since that affects whether materials were cleaned, treated, or need to be removed outright. A written drying log with daily readings is usually what separates a claim that gets approved cleanly from one that gets questioned.
