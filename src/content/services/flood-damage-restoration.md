Flood water behaves differently than a burst supply line. It usually arrives carrying silt, lawn chemicals, or sewage backflow from overwhelmed storm drains, and it often enters through a foundation crack, window well, or low-grade entry point rather than a single pipe. By the time a homeowner notices water on the basement floor, carpet pad, drywall, and insulation may already be wicking moisture several inches above the waterline. The longer that water sits, the more materials cross the line from salvageable to tear-out.

## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood cleanup starts with reading the water, not just removing it. Technicians check for contamination source (storm runoff, sewer backup, groundwater), test for electrical hazards before anyone steps into standing water, and identify which materials can be dried in place versus which need to come out. Most residential flood jobs in Rockville involve basements and lower-level living space, since those are the areas most exposed to grade-level water intrusion and sump pump failure. Extraction equipment pulls bulk water first, then drying equipment (air movers, low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers) runs for several days while moisture content is tracked in the framing, subfloor, and any remaining drywall.

## Our process

1. **Safety and water categorization.** Before extraction starts, we confirm there's no live electrical hazard and classify the water under IICRC S500 guidelines, since flood water from outside the home is frequently Category 2 or 3 and changes how materials are handled.
2. **Extraction and debris removal.** Pumps and extractors remove standing water while crews clear out mud, silt, and debris that floodwater typically deposits along baseboards and under flooring.
3. **Removal of unsalvageable porous materials.** Wet insulation, saturated carpet pad, and drywall that wicked contaminated water get removed rather than dried, since drying contaminated porous material in place just traps the problem behind a clean-looking surface.
4. **Antimicrobial application and structural drying.** Exposed framing and remaining materials are treated and then dried with monitored air movement and dehumidification, with moisture readings logged daily until the structure hits a dry standard.
5. **Documentation for the claim.** Photos, moisture logs, and categorization notes are compiled into a written record that supports the insurance claim and shows the basis for any materials that were removed instead of dried.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating flood water like clean water damage and drying materials that should have been removed. Carpet pad and insulation that sat in contaminated water can hold bacteria and odor long after the surface looks dry, and that shortcut tends to surface weeks later as a smell or a mold colony behind the baseboard. A second mistake is drying too fast and declaring the job done before moisture content in the studs and subfloor has actually normalized, which is one of the first things an insurance adjuster will question if a claim gets reopened. A thorough flood response documents moisture readings over multiple days, not a single visit, and makes a clear, written case for every material that got removed instead of saved.

## What does flood damage restoration cost?

Costs depend on how much water intruded, how contaminated it was, how long it sat before cleanup started, and whether finished materials like drywall, flooring, or trim need to be removed and later rebuilt. Every loss is different, and RestoPros of Central Maryland provides a written scope before any work begins so you know what's being done and why. Standard homeowners insurance often excludes damage from external flooding (water that enters from outside the home) unless you carry a separate flood policy, while water damage from an internal source like a failed sump pump or pipe is more commonly covered, so it's worth checking your policy language with your agent.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Rockville sees its heaviest flood risk during summer thunderstorms and the remnants of tropical systems that track up the Mid-Atlantic, when storm drains and older sump systems can get overwhelmed faster than homeowners expect. Spring snowmelt and prolonged rain stretches also raise the water table in lower-lying neighborhoods, which shows up as basement seepage even without a single major storm. Homes built before modern grading and drainage codes are more prone to repeat flooding at the same entry points, which is worth flagging to your adjuster if this isn't the first incident.
